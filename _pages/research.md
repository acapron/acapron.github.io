</summary>
  

<ul><span style="font-variant: small-caps;">Abstract:</span> Football scores are an imperfect measure of a team's ability, and consequently exaggerate differences in abilities. <h2><b>Working Papers:</b></h2>

<details>
<summary>

<b><a href="/files/ACapron_JMP.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">"No Space Like Home? Selection and Efficiency in Hospital-at-Home"</a></b> (Job Market Paper).

</summary>

<ul><span style="font-variant: small-caps;">Abstract:</span> Hospital capacity is costly to expand, and the value of an available inpatient bed varies with congestion. Hospital-at-Home (HaH) provides inpatient-level care in patients’ residences and can release hospital beds without requiring permanent capacity expansion. However, this only occurs when the hospital offers HaH and the patient accepts. I study how these decisions jointly determine the use and value of flexible capacity. Using clinical, accounting, census, and staffing data from four hospitals along with a novel patient survey, I model and estimate hospital offers and patient acceptance, accounting for time-invariant preferences and endogenous offer selection. Hospitals balance financial returns, clinical needs, and capacity relief while valuing access for patients who prefer home care. At the two largest hospitals, the estimated private value of released capacity is equivalent to <span>$</span>3,410–<span>$</span>4,660 per discounted bed-day at median occupancy. A counterfactual 10<span>%</span> reimbursement increase for HaH care expands potential inpatient bed-days released by 18.5<span>%</span> and raises expected value to patients from access to HaH by 19.6<span>%</span>. Although induced transfers increase treatment costs more than reimbursement, higher payments and filling 50–100<span>%</span> of released bed-days increase hospital profits by <span>$</span>3.3–<span>$</span>6.2 million. These findings show how consumer participation and provider incentives jointly shape the deployment and economic value of flexible hospital capacity.</ul>

</details>

<details>
<summary>

<b><a href="/files/CHMPT_VATE.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">"Dynamic Treatment Choices and Selection into RCTs: Evidence from Prostate Cancer"</a></b> with Barton Hamilton, Brian McManus, Juan Pantano, and Justin Trogdon. 

</summary>

<ul><span style="font-variant: small-caps;">Abstract:</span> We study how voluntary participation and treatment protocols affect the interpretation of randomized controlled trials (RCTs) comparing existing medical treatments. When patients can obtain their preferred treatment outside the trial, those closer to indifference between treatment strategies are more likely to participate. When treatment is dynamic, protocols governing when patients may switch from their initially assigned treatment affect both enrollment and potential outcomes. We develop and estimate a dynamic model of trial participation and treatment choices using aggregate data on volunteers and eligible non-volunteers from a prostate cancer study. Selection operates primarily through patients' preferences over treatment side effects rather than differences in treatment effectiveness. Under the observed protocol, estimated volunteer and population treatment effects are similar for 10-year prostate cancer mortality but differ for metastasis and 15-year mortality. Alternative switching protocols change both participation and treatment effects.</ul>
                                                    
</details>

<details>
<summary>

<b><a href="/files/CHHL_InsiderCEOs.pdf" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">"What do Boards Know? The Value of CEO Selection and Monitoring"</a></b> with Barton Hamilton, Andrés Hincapié, and Noah Lyman, <i>submitted</i>.

</summary>
  
<ul><span style="font-variant: small-caps;">Abstract:</span> How much do boards know about their CEOs, and what is that information worth? We estimate a dynamic model of CEO succession in which boards screen internal and external candidates and monitor the incumbent's evolving quality. Boards' information is valuable but limited: screening resolves about sixty percent of their uncertainty about an internal candidate before appointing one, but little of their uncertainty about an external candidate. Making hiring and firing uninformed would lower firm value by 9.6 and 7.2 percent, respectively. Full information would add another 23 percent. Insiders’ performance advantage at appointment reflects what boards know, not better candidate pools.</ul>

<details>
<summary>
  
<b>"Patient Outcomes with Hospital-at-Home in Routine Care: Evidence from a Spatial Eligibility Boundary"</b> with Bailey Beauclair, Escher Howard-William, Brian McManus, Carlton Moore, and Patrick O’Shea, <i>submitted</i>.

</summary>

<ul><span style="font-variant: small-caps;">Abstract:</span> Trial evidence on Hospital-at-Home (HaH) is generally favorable, but causal evaluation in routine care is difficult because treatment reflects clinical screening, operational constraints, geographic eligibility, and patient acceptance. We study patients with HaH-eligible conditions treated at four hospitals in a large southeastern U.S. health system from September 2021 through February 2026. Using a fuzzy spatial regression discontinuity design, we exploit the program's geographic eligibility boundary, which generated a modest but precisely estimated increase in HaH receipt. We estimate that geographic eligibility reduced mortality during the index episode but increased 30-day inpatient readmission and 90-day post-discharge mortality among patients near the eligibility boundary. Emergency department visits without inpatient admission declined modestly, indicating that the increase in downstream utilization was concentrated in inpatient readmissions. We found fewer imaging and consultation orders and suggestive evidence of fewer falls, but no clear effects on 30-day post-discharge mortality, discharge disposition, or length of stay. Estimates of the effect of HaH receipt were not statistically significant at either post-discharge mortality horizon. Complementary propensity-score matching and doubly robust analyses yielded different estimates even within the same local geographic samples. Among patients offered HaH, these methods produced estimates suggesting fewer readmissions, less institutional post-acute care, and fewer clinical orders, but longer stays and more falls. The contrast highlights the importance of treatment selection and of the distinct populations and identifying assumptions represented by observational and quasi-experimental estimands. Overall, HaH’s effects may differ across stages of care, underscoring the need to evaluate performance across both the index episode and subsequent follow-up rather than through the index stay alone.</ul>  

</details>
